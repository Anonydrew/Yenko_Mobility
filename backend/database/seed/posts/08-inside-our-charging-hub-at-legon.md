It's 9:30 pm at the Yenko hub near the University of Ghana's Legon campus, and the room hums with 180 chargers. Esi, who leads the night shift, looks up from a tablet. "Forty-two bikes under 30%," comes the verdict. "Let's go."

## Why we swap batteries

Most bike-sharing schemes charge their e-bikes by collecting them in a van. That means fewer bikes available, more vehicles on the road and more emissions.

Every Yenko E1 has a **swappable battery** instead. Our team brings charged batteries to the bikes, not the other way round. A swap takes about 40 seconds, and the bike never leaves its parking zone.

## A night on shift

The night shift runs from 9 pm to 5 am, with eight people working in pairs.

**9:30 pm: planning the route.** Our software flags every bike below 30% charge and plans the most efficient route between them, prioritising the parking zones that will be busiest in the morning.

**10 pm: out on the cargo trikes.** Each pair sets off on an electric cargo trike carrying up to 24 charged batteries. The trikes are charged at the hub too, so the whole operation runs without a drop of fuel.

**10 pm to 3 am: swap, check, repeat.** At each bike, the team swaps the battery, checks the brakes, tyres and lights, and wipes down the handlebars and seat. Anything that needs more than a five-minute fix gets a red tag, and the bike goes back to the hub for repair.

**3:30 am: back at base.** Used batteries go onto the chargers. A full charge takes about four hours, so they're ready for the next round.

**5 am: final check.** Before handing over to the morning team, the shift lead confirms every zone has enough charged bikes for the morning rush.

## Powered by the sun

In March we installed a **42-panel solar canopy** on the hub roof. On a sunny day it covers about 35% of our charging needs. The rest comes from the grid, and we're working with our landlord to add battery storage so more of our overnight charging can run on stored solar power.

## A long life for every battery

Each battery has a chip that tracks its health. After about 800 charge cycles, when it can no longer give riders the range they expect, it retires from bike duty. It doesn't go to landfill. We're piloting a programme with a local renewable energy partner to reuse retired batteries as storage for solar street lights in nearby communities.
